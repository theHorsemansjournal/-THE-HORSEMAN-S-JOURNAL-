Common Human Mistakes with Horses

Foundational · Article 5 of 15

Most horses do not have problems. Most horses have people — people with tension they are not aware of, with plans the horse can feel but not understand. The first mistake is arriving without arriving. You walk into the arena still living somewhere else. You bring your body but not yourself. The horse feels the absence immediately.

You cannot bring the noise of your day into the arena and then wonder why the horse is unsettled. The horse is reading you. Read yourself first.
Arriving Without Arriving

Take a few deep breaths before you enter the pasture. Let go of the argument you had this morning. Leave your phone behind. Arrive fully, or do not arrive at all. The horse will know the difference.

Escalating Into the Problem

When the horse says no, the answer is rarely to ask louder. Most often, the answer is to ask smaller. Reward the try before it is perfect. That is the only formula that works. I have watched people get into fights with horses because they kept increasing pressure. The horse became more resistant. The human became more aggressive. The situation turned ugly for no reason except that neither side would back down.

Blaming the Horse for Being a Horse

Horses are prey animals. They spook. They run. They are sensitive to pressure. These are not character flaws. These are survival mechanisms. When you get angry at a horse for being afraid, you are asking it to go against sixty million years of evolution. That is unfair and it will not work. Instead, thank the horse for being honest. Its fear tells you something about the environment or about your own state. Listen to that message.

Inconsistency

You allow something one day and punish it the next. The horse cannot learn in that chaos. Horses need clear, predictable responses. If you want the horse to stand still while you mount, enforce that rule every single time. If you sometimes let it walk off and sometimes correct it, the horse will never understand what you want. Consistency is kindness. Inconsistency is cruelty, even if unintended.

Forgetting That the Horse Is Always Learning

Every interaction is a lesson. Even when you are not training, the horse is watching you. It is learning whether you are safe, whether you are fair, whether you are predictable. Be the person you want the horse to see, even when you think no one is watching. The horse is always watching. And it remembers.

Correcting these mistakes will transform your horsemanship. It is not about learning new techniques. It is about changing how you show up. Be present. Be clear. Be consistent. Be patient. The horse will meet you there.
